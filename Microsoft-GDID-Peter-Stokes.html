How one device ID unmasked the hacker

U.S. v. Peter Stokes · N.D. Ill. 25 CR 812 — a walkthrough of the single thread that ties an anonymous intrusion to a named person: the Microsoft GDID.

Beat 1 of 6

MECHANICS Where does a GDID come from, and how did the FBI get it?

A Global Device ID is generated by Windows itself. When Windows is installed on a machine — physical or virtual — it mints a unique identifier for that installation. Per the affidavit, it is persistent: it survives operating-system updates and follows the device over time. It is tied to the install, not the hardware or the person — reinstalling Windows (same box or different) produces a brand-new GDID, and one person can have several. · ¶25

Because it’s a Microsoft identifier, it lives in Microsoft’s own records, alongside the IP addresses and pages that installation was seen touching across Microsoft services. Investigators didn’t extract it from the hacker’s machine — they obtained it the way the rest of this case was built: through legal process served on providers. The affidavit describes Microsoft criminal referrals and a court order (a reverse §2703(d) order) compelling Microsoft to search its records for accounts and IPs tied to the identifiers of interest. · ¶7, ¶10, ¶16 n.9

THE CASE Does this prove Stokes did everything on one Windows machine?

No — and this is the most common misread. The GDID proves something narrower and more specific:

  • It ties one Windows installation to the creation of the ngrok account and to browsing the victim and proxy — the acts the affidavit pins to the device. ¶25–27
  • It ties that same installation to Stokes personally, via repeated IP overlap with his private accounts. ¶28–30

What it does not claim: that a single laptop committed the whole two-year campaign. Scattered Spider is a group; the affidavit leans on servers, co-conspirator chats, and other victims too. And recall the GDID’s own limits — a Windows reinstall spawns a new ID, so the device here is one instrument in the toolkit, not proof that all activity ran through it. Note too that the intrusion used a rented server and a VPN; the Windows device is what was controlling that infrastructure at key moments, not necessarily where every byte of the attack lived. · ¶8, ¶14, ¶25 n.23

What the GDID supports

“This specific device opened the attack account, looked at the victim, and belongs to Stokes.” A strong link between one machine, the crime, and the man.

What it doesn’t

“Every act in the conspiracy happened on this one Windows box.” The case rests on many sources; the GDID is the connective thread, not the whole rope.

THE CASE The whole point of a VPN is anonymity. Why didn’t it protect him?

This is the conceptual heart of the case, and a clean thing to show. The VPN hid one layer — the network address the victim saw. It did nothing about the device-level identifier Windows was reporting to Microsoft underneath it. So there were two parallel trails, and only one was masked:

The victim’s logs stop at the proxy. Microsoft’s records don’t — they record the device behind it, and that device kept the same fingerprint whether he was attacking Company F or logging into a game from his couch in Tallinn.

That’s why the VPN failed him: it protected the channel he was thinking about and left untouched the one he wasn’t. Investigators simply correlated the unmasked device trail with the crime on one side and with his personal life on the other. · ¶27–30

PRIVACY What does this mean for everyone else?

This case is a clean, real-world illustration of a tension worth naming plainly — it cuts in two directions.

Why it’s powerful and, here, arguably a public good: a persistent device identifier let investigators pierce sophisticated anonymity and attribute a $100M-scale criminal campaign to a specific person, using records lawfully compelled from a provider. Without something like it, the proxy would likely have been a dead end.

Why it should give everyone pause: the same identifier exists on ordinary devices too. Consider what the case quietly reveals about a normal Windows machine:

  • A persistent, device-level ID can follow your installation across services and over years, surviving updates. ¶25
  • Correlated with IP history, it can place a device — and by extension a person — in specific cities on specific dates. Here it reconstructed international travel from a hotel-website visit. ¶28
  • It can link accounts you never connected yourself — a work login and a video-game login collapse into “same device” once the identifier and IPs line up. ¶29

The lawful-process point is doing a lot of work here. What made this legitimate wasn’t that the data couldn’t reveal all this (it clearly can) but that access ran through court orders and criminal referrals. The privacy question is really: a capability this precise exists on billions of consumer devices; the safeguard is the legal process gating who can reach it.

A fair one-line framing: the tool that caught him is the same tool that watches everyone — the line between the two is who’s allowed to ask for the records, and under what authority.

One-line takeaway: the GDID is the only object that touches all three zones — it opened the attack account, cased the victim, and shared IP addresses with Stokes’s personal accounts five times. That overlap is the bridge from an anonymous proxy to a named person. This is a probable-cause affidavit (sworn 16 Apr 2026), so these are the affiant’s assessments, not adjudicated findings.
